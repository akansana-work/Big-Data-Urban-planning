## Important note on the data

The ward-level dataset used in the analysis is modelled, not measured. It is generated by `src/data_generation.py`: for each of 34 real Pune ward names, values are drawn at random from ranges set by a hand-assigned ward type (for example commercial, it_hub, residential_planned). Only the ward names are real. The pipeline (cleaning, scaling, PCA, K-Means) is data-agnostic and works unchanged on real data.

Say this plainly if asked. The dissertation names CPCB, Census and OpenTraffic as sources, and the declaration page says nothing was fabricated, so a hidden fact would look like misconduct while a stated limit looks like honesty.

## Part 2: 10-minute speaking script

### Slide 1: Problem Statement (about 2 min 30 s)

Good morning, respected examiners. My name is Aditya Kansana, and my dissertation is titled *Big Data-driven Strategies for Urban Planning and Development*, with Pune as the case study. I worked under the guidance of Mr. Abhishek Kumar Gupta.

Let me begin with the problem. Cities like Pune are growing very fast. More people, more vehicles and more buildings mean more pressure on roads, air quality and green space. But urban planning has not kept pace. I see three issues.

First, planning is static. It depends on periodic surveys and manual records, which are often outdated by the time a decision is made.

Second, analysis is siloed. Traffic, pollution, population density, infrastructure and green space are usually studied separately, so planners never see the full picture of a ward.

Third, these pressures compound. In a crowded ward, congestion, air pollution and loss of green space tend to rise together.

This leads to my research question, shown on the slide: how can Big Data techniques be used systematically to support zoning, traffic, infrastructure and environmental decisions?

My aim was to build a Big Data-driven framework that analyses urban conditions and supports planning, using open-source tools. I set five objectives. First, review the literature. Second, acquire and preprocess Pune datasets. Third, apply spatial and statistical analysis. Fourth, visualise density, congestion, pollution and green cover. And fifth, propose data-driven strategies for each type of zone.

The scope is Pune at ward level, across five data domains. I did not model water or power systems in detail, and I did not simulate policy outcomes.

### Slide 2: Current Scenario (about 2 min 30 s)

Now, the current scenario. What does existing research tell us?

Batty and colleagues, in 2012, described cities as webs of spatial and temporal data flows. Kitchin, in 2014, introduced the smart city idea, where real-time data guides decisions. Yuan and colleagues used taxi GPS data to find functional zones in Beijing. Liu and colleagues used K-Means clustering to classify land use. And Sahai and colleagues mapped air-quality hotspots in Delhi.

These are valuable, but each studies one dimension: mobility, land use or pollution. None joins population, traffic, pollution, infrastructure and green cover at ward level for a Tier-2 Indian city. That is the research gap I address.

On the right of the slide is the baseline picture from my analysis of 34 Pune wards. About nine of them show a pattern of high stress. Compared with the other wards, these have much higher population density, roughly 20,400 people per square kilometre against about 13,600. Their congestion index is 78.6 against about 43. PM2.5 is 59.8 against 35. Green cover is only 14.5% against about 22%. They are also noisier.

I want to be open about one point. The ward-level dataset I used is a modelled dataset, built because real-time ward-level access was restricted. So these numbers show how my framework behaves, not measured Pune conditions. The pipeline itself is data-agnostic, and real data can replace the inputs without changing the method.

### Slide 3: Research Conclusion (about 3 min)

Now the method and conclusion. The process has five steps, shown in the strip across the slide.

Step one is collecting data across the five domains. Step two is cleaning and scaling. I fill missing values with the median and apply min-max scaling so every feature sits between 0 and 1. This matters because population density is in the thousands and congestion is on a 0 to 100 scale, and without scaling the larger numbers would dominate the clustering.

Step three is feature engineering. I created population density, a pollution load index that adds PM2.5, PM10 and NO2, infrastructure density, and green cover percentage.

Step four is modelling. I first applied Principal Component Analysis, which compresses correlated features; four components kept 94.6% of the variance. Then I applied K-Means clustering with three clusters. K-Means picks centres, assigns each ward to the nearest one, moves each centre to the mean of its wards, and repeats until stable.

Step five is mapping the results and turning them into recommendations.

Figure 1 shows congestion against PM2.5 across the wards. The upward trend gives a correlation of 0.68, and 0.80 with the air quality index. Wards with heavier traffic tend to have worse air. I am careful here: this is association, not proof that traffic causes pollution, since weather and industry matter too.

Figure 2 compares the clusters. The orange cluster is the high-stress group: highest congestion, highest PM2.5, lowest green cover. The other two clusters are residential. One is greener with lower incomes, the other has higher incomes.

My recommendations follow from this. In the high-stress core, add greenery such as vertical gardens and rooftop planting, limit growth of private vehicles and expand air monitoring. In the greener outer wards, add schools, clinics and transit while protecting green cover. In the higher-income residential wards, invest in cycling and walking paths and make targeted fixes rather than large projects.

For validity, the silhouette score is 0.52 for two clusters and 0.30 for three. I chose three because planners need more than two categories, but I accept that the separation between the two residential clusters is moderate.

### Slide 4: Further Scope (about 2 min)

Finally, further scope. Each next step answers a limitation of this study.

First, real data. I already wrote loaders for CPCB, Census, OpenStreetMap and Bhuvan data, so the next step is running the same pipeline on those sources. That addresses the main limitation.

Second, live data streams, such as mobility traces, smart-meter logs and citizen sentiment, to move from a static snapshot to continuous monitoring.

Third, forecasting with LSTM or ARIMA models to predict ward-level stress under different growth scenarios.

Fourth, stronger validation: comparing different values of k, and algorithms such as DBSCAN and hierarchical clustering, on more wards.

Fifth, scaling up with a dashboard in Power BI or on the web, Apache Spark for larger data, and extension to other Indian cities.

To summarise my contributions: a reproducible ward-level framework that joins five urban domains; evidence of how stress zones separate and how congestion and air quality move together; and a fully open-source approach with no licence cost, which makes it practical for municipalities.

The future of urban planning lies in using data to build cities that are smarter, more livable, resilient and equitable. Thank you. I am happy to take your questions.

## Part 3: Viva questions and answers

### A. Easy-going examiner

**1. Explain your project in one minute.**
Pune is growing faster than traditional planning can follow. I built a framework that joins five urban areas at ward level: population, traffic, pollution, infrastructure and green cover. It groups wards by urban stress using clustering, and gives each group a planning strategy.

**2. Why did you choose this topic?**
Cities generate more data than planners use. Most studies look at one dimension, such as traffic or pollution. Pune is a Smart City, so it is a relevant case, and the tools I used are free.

**3. What is Big Data?**
Data defined by volume, velocity, variety and veracity. In urban planning it comes from GPS, satellites, sensors, census records and citizen feedback.

**4. What is your research question?**
How can Big Data be used systematically to support zoning, traffic, infrastructure and environmental decisions?

**5. What tools did you use?**
Python with Pandas for cleaning, GeoPandas for spatial joins, scikit-learn for PCA and K-Means, Folium and Matplotlib for visuals, and Jupyter for analysis.

**6. What is clustering?**
Grouping similar items without labels. Here, wards with similar density, congestion, pollution, infrastructure and green cover go into the same group.

**7. What did you find?**
Nine of the 34 wards form a high-stress group, with congestion 78.6 against about 43 elsewhere and PM2.5 59.8 against 33 to 37. Congestion and PM2.5 correlate at r = 0.68.

**8. What are your recommendations?**
Core wards need greening, limits on new private vehicles and more air monitoring. Outer green wards need schools, clinics and transit while keeping their green cover. Higher-income residential wards need cycling and walking paths.

**9. What is the contribution of your work?**
A reproducible, open-source, ward-level pipeline that combines five domains for a Tier-2 Indian city.

**10. What would you do next?**
Plug in real feeds (CPCB, Census, OSM), add forecasting with LSTM or ARIMA, and build a public dashboard.

### B. Method questions (medium)

**11. Why K-Means?**
It is simple, fast, interpretable and standard for zoning work (Liu 2017). It suits numeric features and gives groups planners can act on.

**12. How did you choose k = 3?**
I tested k = 2 to 8 using elbow, silhouette and Calinski-Harabasz scores. Silhouette is highest at k = 2 (0.52); at k = 3 it is 0.30. I chose three because planners need more than two categories. I accept the trade-off in cluster separation.

**13. How does K-Means work?**
Pick k centres. Assign each ward to the nearest centre. Move each centre to the mean of its wards. Repeat until assignments stop changing.

**14. Why normalise the features?**
K-Means uses Euclidean distance. Population density is in the thousands and congestion is 0 to 100, so without scaling the large-unit features would dominate. Min-max scaling puts all on 0 to 1.

**15. What does PCA do here?**
It compresses correlated features into fewer components. Four components kept 94.6% of the variance.

**16. What features did you engineer?**
Population density (population divided by area), pollution load (PM2.5 + PM10 + NO2), infrastructure density (amenities divided by area), green cover (green area divided by ward area).

**17. What is a spatial join?**
Merging tables with geometry using a shared key, here the ward ID, so each metric attaches to its ward polygon.

**18. How did you handle missing values?**
Median imputation for numbers and most-frequent for categories. The dissertation also mentions a 24-hour rolling mean for AQI gaps.

**19. What is silhouette score?**
For each point, it compares distance to its own cluster against the nearest other cluster. It ranges from -1 to 1; higher means better separation.

**20. What does r = 0.68 mean?**
Pearson correlation. Moderately strong, positive. Wards with higher congestion tend to have higher PM2.5. It does not prove that traffic causes pollution; weather and industry also matter.

### C. Strict, experienced examiner

**21. Is your data real?**
No. The ward-level dataset is modelled from assumed ranges per ward type, because ward-level real-time access was restricted. The pipeline is data-agnostic: real CPCB, Census and OSM data can replace the inputs with no change to the method. My results show how the framework behaves, not measured Pune conditions.

**22. Then your findings are meaningless.**
The findings are not measurements of Pune. They demonstrate that the framework recovers structure and produces zone-specific outputs. The contribution is the method and pipeline. The next step is rerunning on real data.

**23. Your dissertation lists CPCB, Census and OpenTraffic as sources. Did you use them?**
I designed the pipeline around those sources and wrote loaders for them. The reported analysis ran on a modelled dataset because access was restricted. The dissertation should have said so more clearly, and I acknowledge that. (This question decides the viva. Answer plainly and briefly.)

**24. Your clusters could just reflect how you generated the data.**
Yes, that is a limit. Commercial and dense wards were given higher traffic and pollution ranges, so the high-stress cluster is partly built in. Real data is needed to confirm it.

**25. Silhouette is 0.52 for k = 2 and 0.30 for k = 3. Why use k = 3?**
Statistically, k = 2 separates cleanly: high-stress against the rest. k = 3 splits the rest into two residential groups that are close on traffic and pollution and differ mainly on income and green cover. I chose three for planning granularity, but the evidence for the second split is weak.

**26. Why not DBSCAN or hierarchical clustering?**
K-Means was enough for a first framework with a fixed number of planning categories. DBSCAN suits irregular shapes and noise but needs density tuning. Comparing algorithms is in my further scope.

**27. How stable are your clusters?**
I fixed random_state 42 with n_init 10. I did not run a formal stability test, such as resampling or comparing seeds. That is a limitation.

**28. What are the weaknesses of K-Means?**
You must choose k, it is sensitive to outliers and initialisation, and it assumes roughly spherical clusters.

**29. Does a correlation of 0.68 on 34 points mean much?**
It is moderate. Sample size is small, so the confidence interval is wide, and spatial autocorrelation between neighbouring wards can inflate it. It suggests association only.

**30. You say "Big Data". Your dataset has 34 rows per table. Is that Big Data?**
By size, no. The Big Data character is variety and integration: five domains in CSV, JSON and geospatial formats. The pipeline is modular and scales to larger inputs, for example with Spark, which is in my further scope.

**31. Your dissertation mentions Spark, QGIS, Power BI and Tableau. Did you use them?**
No. The implementation used Python libraries. Those tools are the scale-up and dashboard path.

**32. How did you validate your results?**
Internal metrics only: silhouette, Calinski-Harabasz and explained variance. I did not validate against ground truth or independent data. That is a limitation.

**33. How would you validate against reality?**
Compare cluster assignments with real station readings, census data and planner judgement, and test stability across time periods.

**34. What is the novelty over Yuan, Liu and Sahai?**
Each covers one dimension: mobility, land use or pollution. I combine five domains at ward level in an open-source framework for a Tier-2 Indian city.

**35. Where is the real-time capability you claim?**
The framework supports it, but the reported analysis used a static dataset. Live feeds are in further scope. I should not describe the current work as real-time.

**36. Why min-max and not z-score?**
Min-max keeps everything on 0 to 1, which suits a composite index. Z-score would be better for outliers; testing both is a fair extension.

**37. Do your recommendations follow from the data?**
They follow from cluster profiles, for example lowest green cover and highest congestion in the core. They are suggestions, not tested policy; I did not simulate policy outcomes.

**38. Your dissertation says Aundh and Baner are balanced, but your clusters put them in the high-stress group.**
Correct. The dissertation text and the final run differ there. In the final run, high congestion and amenity density place them in the stress cluster. I would correct the dissertation to match the results.

**39. What would you change if you started again?**
Use real OpenStreetMap, Census and air-quality data from the start, run a stability analysis, and compare clustering algorithms.

### D. How to answer
- Be short: two or three sentences, then stop.
- Say "that is a limitation" early. Examiners score honesty above defensiveness.
- Do not argue with a correct criticism. Agree, then give the fix.
- For anything you do not know, say "I did not test that, I would approach it by..." and name the method.
- Practise questions 21 to 25 aloud. They decide how a strict examiner sees you.
